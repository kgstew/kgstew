## Headline options — considered (kept for the record)

### A. "I connect things that don't talk to each other."
Literally true of the WMD integration API, the butterfly mesh, the Fable Bound control
chain, and the hacktivations — and it covers software, hardware, *and* people in one
sentence. Memorable, and it's a hook rather than a summary.
**Risk:** slightly riddle-like; it needs the line under it to land.

### B. "I design systems and assemble the people who build them."
Unambiguous. States both halves — technical architecture and human orchestration. Reads
cleanly to a hiring manager or a client who found you cold.
**Risk:** less distinctive, and "assemble the people" edges toward management-speak.

### C. "I make separate parts work as one thing."
Plain and short. Works equally for modules, services, and crews.
**Risk:** this is the draft-1 failure mode again — true but doesn't point at anything.

### D. "Give me a system nobody has explained and a group of people, and I'll get it built."
Most voice, captures the unfamiliarity part you named in batch 1 Q2.
**Risk:** long for a headline, and the conditional construction is weaker than a statement.

**My recommendation: A as the headline, B as the line directly beneath it.** Hook, then
clarity. Everything else on the page is evidence.

## The origin story

This is the most valuable thing you've told me and you dropped it in a **[quick]** question:

> "I got into software in 2013 when one of the founders of Zenput, who is a good friend of
> mine, offered me a job as a junior software engineer. Knowing that I had never written
> software before, he believed in me and said that I would figure it out. There we go: ten
> years later, I have a career as a software engineer and as a software engineering manager."

You have been saying — in batch 1 Q5, in batch 2 Q10 about Matt — that people are capable
of far more than managers give them credit for, and that you hand people things they don't
yet know they can do. On its own that reads as a nice sentiment. **With this, it reads as
someone paying back the exact thing that happened to him.** That's what makes the claim
credible instead of boastful.

Draft:

> In 2013 a friend offered me a job as a junior software engineer. I had never written a
> line of code. He figured I'd work it out. Ten years later I was managing engineering
> teams.
>
> That's most of what I believe about people. Matt showed up to a build day not knowing
> anyone, having never made his own art. He learned to solder, learned to weld, became the
> person I handed the hard problems to, and is now moving into fabrication as a career.
>
> Both of those are the same story. It's why the projects on this site are almost never
> mine alone.

**Question for you:** is the 2013 story on the homepage, on the about page, or too personal
for either? I think it's the most persuasive paragraph available to you, but it's yours to
place.

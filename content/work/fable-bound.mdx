## Requirement

The ship had to move on demand, in time with music, with people standing on it, for a week in the
desert. That set most of the architecture. The movement needed enough force to lift a loaded hull,
a slow enough travel to be safe with people aboard, and control that could be handed to whoever was
running the music rather than to an operator with a switch.

## Movement

Four shop air jacks are ganged under the hull, the type used for lifting trucks. Each is rated at
3,500 lb, giving roughly fourteen inches of vertical travel at the bow. Air is valved by twelve-volt
solenoids driven through relays on a board Nathan Koral built for the project.

## Control

Firmware on the board handles three relay functions and holds the safety interlocks. Fill and
exhaust do what they say. The third drives a secondary ballast reserve, which keeps air available
between cycles so a movement never has to wait on the compressor catching up.

Above that, a Mac mini runs a Node application that receives MIDI. Each note maps to a movement
program. Sequencing the ship from the same Ableton session that played the music meant the person
running sound was also running the ship, and neither side needed to understand the other.

I designed the system and did not write most of that application. I refactored it afterwards to make
it maintainable.

## Safety

The piece was built for Burning Man, where the standard is lower than it would be for a permanent
public installation. Pinch points were made physically unreachable rather than signposted, and the
stairs and deck were built so that climbing aboard felt adventurous without offering anyone a way to
get it wrong. It has not been installed in a non-Burning Man public context, which would need
constant on-site oversight.

## Field failure

The compressor charging the pneumatic system failed on the third day and could not be replaced on
playa. Lighting and sound ran for the full week; the movement did not. The system itself was sound —
the failure was in the supply feeding it. It was a straightforward repair once we were home, and the
ship has run at two decompression events since.

## Crew

Eleven months of build days, and about half the crew found the project through the Burning Man
artist posting rather than through anyone on it. Martin Stenflo and Leanne Lopez made the shields,
each one different.

Matt arrived at a build day knowing nobody. He had worked on other people's art and never made his
own. He learned to solder, learned to weld, and became the person I handed the hard problems to. He
is moving into fabrication as a career now, and building installations in Denver with other people
from this crew.
